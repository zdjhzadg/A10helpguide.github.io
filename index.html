Section 1

Cold start

The Hog wakes up in one order and only one. Skip a step and the next one refuses — the aircraft tells you which, in the label of the switch that will not move.

  1. Battery on. Nothing else on the aircraft answers until the bus is live — every other switch stays greyed out.
  2. Inverter on. It feeds the igniters. Start an engine without it and you flood the combustion chamber instead of lighting it.
  3. APU generator to PWR. Set before the APU turns, so its power is on the bus the moment it comes up to speed. This is the step people forget.
  4. Ejection seat armed. Do it on the ramp, with the engines still quiet — an unarmed seat is a decoration, and you will not remember it later.
  5. APU start. It takes about twenty seconds to come up to speed. Watch its gauge settle before going further.
  6. Engine start. They spool one after the other, not together, and the TF34 is a slow engine — give it the better part of a minute.
  7. Canopy closed. Independent of the rest, and easy to forget on a hot ramp.
Note

Every switch label shows where it is now, not what the click will do. Battery: ON means the battery is on. When a step is blocked the reason follows in brackets — APU: OFF (battery required).

Section 2

Electrical system

The whole aircraft hangs off one switch, then off three possible sources. Knowing which one is actually carrying the bus tells you what will still be alive after the next thing breaks.

BatteryThe master switch. Off, the aircraft is a lump of metal — no screens, no lights, and no other switch on the panel will move. On, it feeds the bus on its own, and nothing else does.
InverterMakes AC out of battery power. It feeds the igniters, and the APU will not start without it. It refuses to be switched off while the APU is running — the APU is drawing from it. It only feeds the essential buses: the MFCDs are not on it.
APU generatorPuts the APU's output on the bus. It is what the starters run on, so the engines will not turn without it — and it will not switch off while an engine is running.
AC generators, left and rightOne per engine, and the only source that survives once the APU is shut down. If neither is on PWR when the APU goes, everything on the bus goes with it.
Bleed airEngine air for the systems that need it. On the ground it is the APU that supplies it, which is the other reason the APU has to be running before the engines will start.
  1. Once both engines are running, put AC GEN L and AC GEN R to PWR. Do it before anything else on this panel.
  2. Then, and only then, shut the APU down and take the APU generator off. In that order — the aircraft will not let you do it the other way round while an engine is turning.
  3. The inverter can come off once the APU is gone. It exists to start the aircraft, not to fly it.
  4. At shutdown, work backwards: APU running and its generator on before you drop the AC generators, then engines, then the rest.
Caution

The MFCDs live on the main AC buses — left screen on the left bus, right screen on the right. Those buses are fed by the APU generator with the APU running, or by an AC generator on PWR with its engine turning; one generator is enough for both screens. Battery and inverter alone keep the essentials alive, not the screens. Forgetting this is how the MFCDs go black halfway through an attack run.

Note

Switching the battery off in flight saves nothing. It kills the bus, the screens and the whole panel; the engines keep running, but you are now flying an aeroplane you can no longer aim.

Section 3

Fuel system

Four tanks inside, two engines, and two circuits that never talk to each other unless you tell them to. Nearly all of it is set once before taxi — the exceptions are what bring you home.

  1. Four internal tanks: a wing tank and a fuselage main on each side. The left engine drinks from the left pair, the right engine from the right pair, and nothing crosses on its own.
  2. Each side empties its wing tank first, then its main. That is deliberate: it keeps the weight near the centreline as the fuel goes.
  3. Boost pumps push the fuel up to the engines. Four of them, one per internal tank, and all four are off on a cold aircraft — turning them on is the first thing you do on this panel.
  4. CROSS FEED opens the wall between the two circuits. With it open either engine can drink from any tank, which is the answer to half the problems in this manual.
  5. TK GATE joins the two fuselage mains and levels them by itself. Useful, and also the fastest way to move your centre of gravity if you leave it open and forget about it.
  6. External tanks transfer inwards only while their switch is on. Off, that fuel is ballast you carried all the way to the target for nothing.
  7. FILL DISABLE stops a tank being filled on the boom. Depressed for normal flight; pulled only for a tank you do not want fuel in.
BOOST PUMP MAIN L / RFeeds its own engine from the fuselage tank on that side. Both pumps off on one side and that engine dies, even with a full aircraft.
BOOST PUMP WING L / RSame job for the wing tank, and it is the tank each side empties first.
CROSS FEEDLinks the two circuits. Open it when one engine is dead, or when one side is running dry and the other is not.
TK GATELevels the two fuselage mains between themselves. Open it to fix an imbalance, then close it again.
EXT TKS WING / FUSTransfers from the drop tanks into the internals. On whenever you are carrying them, off when you are not.
FILL DISABLEBlocks refilling of that one tank on the boom. Pull it for a tank that is leaking or that you are deliberately keeping empty.
Note

With every pump off the engines still suck fuel out of the tanks by themselves — but only below 10 000 ft. Climb above that with the pumps off and both engines run down within a minute or two.

Caution

The fuel does not like negative g. Push over hard and hold it and the collectors run empty in about ten seconds; both engines quit, and getting them back is a full restart.

Note

Numbers to plan with: each engine burns roughly 450 litres an hour at idle and 2 600 at full power, the APU costs 60. Drop tanks transfer at about 600 litres a minute; the boom fills you at 2 000.

Section 4

Hydraulic systems

Two systems, one per engine, and they do not share the work evenly. What you lose depends entirely on which one goes, so it is worth knowing the split before you need it.

  1. The left system is driven by the left engine, the right by the right. Neither builds pressure until its engine is actually turning at a working speed.
  2. Normal pressure sits around 3 000 psi. A gauge below about 1 000 is not a low system, it is a failed one — everything on it is gone.
  3. Shut an engine down and its system bleeds off in a few seconds. Relight it and the pressure is back almost as fast.
  4. A leak looks different: the needle drops much faster, and it does not come back when the engine is running. Treat that one as permanent.
Left systemFlaps, nosewheel steering, normal gear extension, wheel brakes and anti-skid, and the left elevator. In other words, most of what you need to land normally.
Right systemSpeed brakes, the refuelling slipway door, the slats, and the right elevator.
Either oneAilerons work on either system. The rudder works on either too, but on one system it gives you roughly half its usual authority.
NeitherManual reversion: cables and muscle. It flies, barely, and it lands once.
Caution

Lose the left system and the landing changes shape: no flaps, no nosewheel steering, no braking you can rely on. Pick the longest runway you can reach, arrive slow, and plan to stop straight ahead.

Note

Lose the right system and the slats come out and stay out. The aircraft gets slower and more forgiving, which is exactly what they are for.

Warning

Both systems out is manual reversion. Fly with the smallest inputs that do the job, keep it close to wings-level, and put it on the ground at the first place that will take it.

Section 5

Taxi and take-off

The Warthog steers with its nosewheel at low speed and with its rudder at high speed, and there is a stretch in between where neither is very convincing. Plan for it.

  1. Nosewheel authority fades as you accelerate and is essentially gone by 70 kt. Below that it turns tightly; above it, lead with rudder.
  2. Brakes pitch the nose down hard. That is real, and it is also your friend on a short field — but nothing happens when you are already stopped.
  3. Push the throttles up and the nose settles a little further: the engines sit high, so power pitches you down. It is small, and it is correct.
  4. Take-off flaps for a loaded jet or a short strip, clean for a light one. Rotate gently; hauling on the stick early only drags the tail.
  5. Gear up once you are climbing and not before — the wheels stay down while there is weight on them anyway.
Caution

Full rudder rolls the aircraft. A big fin and a lot of effective dihedral do that on any real aeroplane: at full pedal you get roughly a quarter of a full aileron input, and nearly twice the drag. Use it to point, not to turn.

Section 6

Flying the Hog

It is honest, slow, and it has no energy to spare. Everything that makes it good at its job makes it bad at going fast, and the two are the same design decision.

  1. Level top speed is about 380 kt clean. If you are seeing much more, you are descending, whatever the attitude indicator suggests.
  2. A hard turn costs you speed you will not get back quickly. At 7 g you are bleeding better than ten knots a second, and the engines take five seconds just to spool.
  3. Stores are what hurt. A heavy jet turns noticeably worse than a clean one — if the fight matters more than the load, drop the load.
  4. Stall is gentle and well announced: it buffets first, then drops a wing. Unload, let it fly, and it recovers on its own.
Note

There is no fly-by-wire here. The dampers steady the aircraft in pitch and yaw and help the turn stay coordinated, but nothing holds an attitude for you and nothing stops you exceeding a limit.

Section 7

Trim

Nose-heavy, tail-heavy, one wing down — the Hog needs trimming constantly, and it is the difference between flying it and fighting it.

  1. Pitch is the one you will use most. Left alone the nose rises; a small amount of nose-down trim holds a cruise for you.
  2. Roll trim answers an uneven load. Drop everything off one wing and the aircraft leans — a touch of roll trim takes it out.
  3. Yaw trim is a knob you set once. It cancels a lasting sideslip — a crosswind approach, or an engine running down.
  4. The reset control zeroes all three at once. Use it before landing rather than trying to unwind each one by hand.
Note

Trim moves the control surface itself, so it works just as well fast as slow. Unlike stick input, it does not get lighter as the air loads build up.

Section 8

Autopilot

Three controls, used in one order. The autopilot is there to fly you to the target area while you work the sensors — it is not a combat aid.

  1. Arm EAC first. Nothing engages without it, and the message tells you so.
  2. Pick a mode. ALT/HDG holds altitude and heading, ALT holds altitude and bank, PATH holds the flight path — where the aircraft is actually going, not where the nose points.
  3. Engage. Wings roughly level: it refuses beyond about ten degrees of bank in the modes that hold a heading or a path.
Caution

EAC has to be armed before anything will engage, and EAC itself refuses if the stability augmentation is out. The aircraft tells you which one — AUTOPILOT INHIBITED, EAC OFF, or EAC INHIBITED, SAS INOPERATIVE.

Section 9

Landing

Slow, stable and unglamorous. The Hog lands well if you let it settle and badly if you fly it onto the runway.

  1. Get rid of the speed early. Flaps come out below about 185 kt and retract themselves if you go faster.
  2. Gear down, then set the attitude and hold it. Aim to arrive with a low rate of descent rather than a precise touchdown point.
  3. Let the nosewheel down of its own accord, then brake. Braking with the nose still up wastes the runway you have left.
  4. Trim reset on short final. Landing an aircraft trimmed for cruise is a fight you do not need.
Caution

Arrive fast and the aircraft skips. The suspension has a limited amount of travel and gives back what it absorbs — a firm, controlled touchdown beats a fast, flat one.

Section 10

When things break

The Warthog is built to come home broken. Nearly every case below has a two- or three-switch answer, and the switches are on panels you have already set once today.

A · Engine failure on take-off

  1. Fly first. Nose down to the speed you had, and get on the rudder — the aircraft swings towards the dead engine and it will keep swinging.
  2. Clean up the drag: gear up, speed brakes in, and get rid of anything you do not need to keep flying.
  3. Yaw trim out the pedal once you are stable. You will be standing on it otherwise, and you still have a circuit to fly.
  4. Open CROSS FEED so the live engine can drink from both sides, then fly a wide, flat circuit back. It will climb. Slowly.

B · Engine quits in the cruise

  1. Check the fuel panel before you blame the engine. A boost pump off on that side, or a side that has run dry, looks exactly like an engine failure.
  2. CROSS FEED open, pumps checked, then try a relight. With the other engine running you do not need the APU for this.
  3. If it will not relight, you are now case A with more time. Trim the yaw out, keep the speed up, and go home.

C · Fuel going unequal

  1. Open CROSS FEED first so both engines are drawing from everywhere, not from one side each.
  2. Turn the pumps off on the light side. The heavy side now feeds both engines and empties first, which is what you want.
  3. TK GATE open levels the two fuselage tanks on its own. Watch it, and close it as soon as they are even.
  4. If one tank keeps refusing to stay level, pull its fill disable and stop feeding it altogether.

D · The screens go dark

  1. It is the AC bus, not the screens. Battery on, then either the APU generator with the APU running, or an AC generator on PWR with its engine turning. The inverter alone does not light them.
  2. The usual cause is shutting the APU down after start without putting the AC generators to PWR first. Put them on and the bus comes straight back.
  3. Give the screens time to run their own start-up before you start pressing things. They come back in their default pages, not the ones you left.

E · Left hydraulic system out

  1. No flaps, no nosewheel steering, no anti-skid, and braking you cannot rely on. The approach has to absorb all of that.
  2. Fly it long and flat and a little faster than usual — you have no flaps to slow you down and none to help you sit down.
  3. Steer with rudder on the roll-out and let it run. Do not stand on the brakes hoping they will bite.

F · Right hydraulic system out

  1. Slats extend and stay out, the speed brakes are gone, and the refuelling door will not open. You have flaps and brakes, which is the important half.
  2. Without speed brakes everything takes longer to slow down. Start the descent earlier and plan a longer final.
  3. Do not plan on air refuelling. The door is on this system and it stays shut.

G · Both hydraulic systems out

  1. Manual reversion. Everything is heavy, everything is late, and the aircraft answers about a second after you ask.
  2. Small inputs, wings near level, shallow turns. Chasing it makes it worse and there is no margin to spend.
  3. Pick somewhere long and land straight in. There is no second approach in this configuration.

H · Both engines run down at altitude

  1. A full aircraft losing both engines above 10 000 ft is a pump problem, not an engine problem.
  2. All four boost pumps on, then restart. If they are already on, descend below 10 000 ft and the engines will feed themselves.
  3. Trade the height for speed while you sort it out. Gliding is the one thing this aircraft is genuinely good at.

I · Both engines quit after a hard push-over

  1. You held negative g too long and the collectors emptied. Get positive g back first — nothing relights while they are dry.
  2. Then restart properly: the battery is still on, so it is inverter, APU, APU generator, engines — the same order as a cold start.
  3. Do it while you still have height. The full sequence is not quick, and you are descending the whole time.
Warning

Nothing in this aircraft stops you overspeeding in a dive, and nothing warns you about it. In a steep dive it will pass any figure you like on the way down; the only limit is the one you fly.

Section 11

Before you shoot

Nothing leaves a pylon until four things are true. Learn them in this order and most of your problems never happen.

  1. Electrical power is up: battery, then the APU generator or an AC generator on line. On battery and inverter alone the MFCDs stay dark, and the armament bus with them.
  2. MASTER ARM is out of SAFE. In SAFE the firing circuit is cut: the gun still fires, nothing else does.
  3. A weapon is selected on the DSMS. Selecting a station is not the same as selecting a weapon type.
  4. The trigger station is on PYLONS, not GUN. Pressing V cycles the vanilla slot; the DSMS switches it for you when you pick a weapon.
Note

The gun is deliberately independent. It is a vanilla weapon slot and answers even when the rest of the armament system is inhibited — which is why "only the GAU-8 fires" is the classic symptom of a selection problem, not a gun problem.

HUD cursor and steerpoints

With the HUD as SOI, the slew drives the TDC, a small green square that starts on the flight path. Release the slew and it stays on the ground point. If it leaves the HUD, it goes back to the flight path. TMS right drops a markpoint under it.

DMS up and down step through the steerpoints: every map marker whose name starts with WP, flight plan and markpoints together. The HUD, the helmet and the TAD all show the same steerpoint. As long as no sensor has made an SPI, the steerpoint is the SPI.

The HUD also shows a TGP diamond where the pod is looking, whenever the TGP is on, warm and in A-G. With the pod in boresight, fly the diamond onto a target and track it: that is the quick draw.

Caution

A switch that seems dead is almost always pressed on the wrong SOI. Look for the asterisk on the helmet or the frame on the MFCD before blaming the switch.

Section 15

TGP — targeting pod

The pod is your eye, your rangefinder and your laser. Two codes live on this page and confusing them is the single most common laser failure.

The two laser codes

OSBLabelRole
18LThe code your own laser emits. Default 1688.
17LSSThe code you search for. Set this to the JTAC's code for buddy lasing.

They are separate on purpose: you can hunt a JTAC's spot on 1511 while your own laser stays on 1688. Merge them and two spots share a code — the weapon can lock the wrong one.

Laser spot search

  1. CNTL (OSB 1) — enter the code you want to find under LSS (OSB 17).
  2. Return to A-G and press OSB 6, or DMS right long with the TGP as SOI. The label goes to [LSS]: searching.
  3. When energy is detected the pod slews to it. One second later the label reads [LST]: locked.
  4. In LST the pod follows the spot on its own and the aircraft seekers retune to that code. It does not become your SPI until you press TMS forward long — exactly like the real pod.
Warning

Codes use digits 1 to 8 only, exactly like real laser codes — 1111 through 1788. A code containing 0 or 9 will never match anything.

SADL — sharing the SPI

The datalink puts your SPI on your friends' helmets and theirs on yours. One long press sends it; nobody has to read coordinates over the radio.

  1. Set your SPI the usual way, from any sensor.
  2. TMS left long, from any SOI. Your SPI is now broadcast. The same press stops it.
  3. Every friendly A-10 sees it on the helmet within a second. It follows your SPI as you move it.
  4. To use someone else's: helmet SOI, crosshair on their symbol, TMS forward short to hook it, TMS forward long to make it your own SPI.
SymbolWhat it tells you
OWNYour own SPI. White, three tiers.
FLIGHTFlight member SPI — a pilot in your own group. Blue, two tiers.
DONORDonor SPI — a friendly pilot outside your group. Green, two tiers.
Note

The server sends each pilot only the SPIs of his own side, once a second. A symbol that has not been refreshed for three and a half seconds disappears, and a broadcast stops by itself when its author leaves the aircraft.

Caution

A shared SPI is where the other pilot's sensor is looking, not a confirmed target. Look at it with your own pod before you release anything on it.

The flight model behind the numbers

Guided stores are flown by the same AFM integrator that computes the launch acceptable region on your HUD — one atmosphere, one drag polar, one set of masses. The zone you are shown and the path the bomb actually takes come out of the same code, so if the LAR says you are in the zone, the bomb agrees.

Note

Release energy is everything for a glider. A GBU-39 dropped low and slow has no range no matter how good the guidance is — the LAR on the HUD is computed from the same physics the bomb flies, so trust it.

Section 19

Attack procedures

Three deliveries cover everything this aircraft does. Learn them as sequences, not as button lists.

A · Gun pass with PAC

  1. GUN/PAC to ARM. Roll in, target under the gun cross.
  2. First detent: the PAC locks the aiming point and holds the aircraft on it.
  3. Second detent: fire. The PAC keeps the pipper on the point against 45 kN of recoil.
  4. Off target by 900 m minimum. The gun outranges your escape, not the debris.

C · CCRP — self-designated GPS

  1. TGP page, A-G. Slew the pod onto the target — or look at it with the helmet as SOI and DMS right long to slave the pod to your eyes.
  2. TMS forward short for a POINT or AREA track so the pod holds it, then TMS forward long — the coordinates are now the SPI. SPI TGP on the IAM page does the same.
  3. Check the station reads RDY, not ALIGN. Alignment takes about 18 seconds from MASTER ARM.
  4. Fly toward the ASL, centre it, and hold the trigger down.
  5. The computer releases when the solution is reached. Releasing the trigger cancels the consent.
Note

Holding the trigger and letting the computer choose the instant is the real CCRP sequence, and it is what separates a guided release from a manual one. In MAN mode the trigger releases immediately instead.

D · Buddy lasing — JTAC designates

A ground controller with a SOFLAM, or a wingman's pod, puts a spot on the target. What you do next depends entirely on what you are carrying.

  2. A-G page, OSB 6 or DMS right long to search. Wait for [LST].
  3. The pod is on the spot. TMS forward long makes it the SPI. For a GPS store the coordinates are then captured — the JTAC can go dark.

Section

Practical cases

Thirteen situations, each worked from the first switch to the moment you know it worked. The chips show the switch and its keyboard default.

Case A

Put the pod where you are looking

You spot something through the canopy and want it on the TGP now.

  1. Helmet on: HMCS Power · Num *. Then Coolie ↑ · RShift+↑ short until the asterisk appears bottom left: the helmet is SOI.
  2. Put the crosshair on the target and hold DMS → · RAlt+→ for a second. The pod swings to your line of sight.
  3. DMS ← · RAlt+← short puts the TGP video on the right MFCD if it is not there already.
  4. Hold Coolie → · RShift+→: the TGP is SOI. TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short to track it, then zoom with DMS ↑ · RAlt+↑.

CheckThe TGP LOS box on the helmet sits on the target, and the TGP page reads AREA or POINT.

Case B

Designate a precise spot with the helmet cursor

The crosshair is too coarse: you want one particular building corner, without moving your head.

  1. Helmet SOI. Move the HDC with Slew · Num 8/2/4/6 until it sits on the spot.
  2. Slew press · Num 5: the HDC sticks to the ground there and stays on it while you look elsewhere.
  3. Hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑: the HDC point is your SPI.
  4. Hold China ↓ · RCtrl+End to send the TGP there. China ↓ · RCtrl+End short recages the HDC when you are done.

CheckThe white three-tier SPI sits on the corner, and the pod shows it.

Case C

Put the pod on a friend's waypoint and attack it

A friend drops a waypoint on the target and it appears on your TAD and helmet. The real A-10 does exactly this with a shared steerpoint.

  1. Make the TAD SOI: hold Coolie ← · RShift+← or Coolie → · RShift+→ toward the TAD page.
  2. TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short until the hook box is on the waypoint. Each press moves to the next friend or waypoint.
  3. Hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑: the waypoint is your SPI. Then hold China ↑ · RCtrl+Home: TGP and Maverick look at it.
  4. A waypoint is where someone clicked, not a target. Make the TGP SOI, find the real target around it, TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short to track it and hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ to make the pod the SPI.
  5. Attack it as a self-designated CCRP: station RDY, centre the ASL, hold the trigger.

CheckYour SPI symbol sits on the waypoint first, then on the pod's point once you refine it.

Case D

Mark a point as you fly over it

You see a mortar pit on the way to something else and want to come back to it.

  1. Helmet SOI, look at it (or put the HDC on it) and press TMS → · RCtrl+→ short. A markpoint appears, named with the next NATO letter.
  2. Carry on with the mission. When you are ready, hold TMS → · RCtrl+→ to make the last markpoint your SPI, then hold China ↓ · RCtrl+End to send the pod to it.
  3. Crosshair on it and Delete markpoint · Num . removes it once the job is done.

CheckThe markpoint shows on the helmet and on the map.

Case E

Hand your target to your wingman

You have the target in your pod; your wingman cannot find it. No coordinates on the radio.

  1. Make the target your SPI: TGP SOI, hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑.
  2. Hold TMS ← · RCtrl+←: you are broadcasting. Tell him: SPI is up.
  3. On his side: helmet SOI, crosshair on your blue (same group) or green symbol, TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short to hook, then hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ to make it his SPI.
  4. He sends his pod there by holding China ↓ · RCtrl+End and looks for himself. Hold TMS ← · RCtrl+← again to stop your broadcast.

CheckHis helmet shows your symbol, and it moves when you move your SPI.

Case F

Identify a vehicle five kilometres out

Friend or foe? You need to see the turret, not a dot.

  1. TGP SOI. Put the pod on it in WIDE, then China ↑ · RCtrl+Home short for NARO.
  2. DMS ↑ · RAlt+↑ short, step by step, up to 9Z. At 5 km the picture is then about 44 m wide.
  3. TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short to hold it. The slew slows down with the zoom, so small corrections stay small.
  4. At night, Boat ↓ · RAlt+End for white-hot; Boat ↑ · RAlt+Home gives black-hot if the scene is washed out.

CheckThe TGP page reads NARO and 9Z.

Case G

Stay on a moving vehicle

A truck is driving away and you keep losing it while you manoeuvre.

  1. TGP SOI, slew the cross onto the truck itself, not the road beside it.
  2. TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short. The track field reads POINT: the pod follows the truck on its own.
  3. It reads AREA instead? Nothing lockable was under the cross. Zoom in and try again.
  4. Hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑: the SPI now moves with the truck. TMS ↓ · RCtrl+↓ short (INR) lets it go.

CheckYou can roll and turn; the picture stays centred on the truck.

Case H

Use the Maverick seeker as a second eye

The pod is busy on one target and you want to watch another.

  1. Power the seeker early: MAV page, OSB 4 to EO ON. It needs three minutes to align, and ignores the HOTAS until then.
  2. Make the MAV page SOI by holding Coolie ← · RShift+← or Coolie → · RShift+→. China ↑ · RCtrl+Home short for NFOV.
  3. Slew onto the target with Slew · Num 8/2/4/6 and let go: the seeker tries to track what is under the cross.
  4. Nothing to lock, like an empty trench? Hold Boat center · RAlt+Ins one second (force correlate); the seeker then holds the ground point.
  5. Hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑: the seeker's point is the SPI, so you can bring the pod to it later.

CheckThe MAV page shows its track cue, and the SPI sits where the seeker looks.

Case I

Find a JTAC's laser without leaving the stick

The JTAC gives you his code and lases; you want the spot as your SPI.

  1. Enter his code under LSS (TGP, CNTL, OSB 17) before the run. LASER ARM to ARM.
  2. TGP SOI on the A-G page, hold DMS → · RAlt+→: the label goes to [LSS].
  3. Point the pod roughly at the area. When it finds the spot it reads [LST] and follows it.
  4. Hold TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑: the spot is your SPI. The pod does not do this for you, and neither does the real one.

CheckCall SPOT. Your SPI sits on his spot, on the HUD and on the helmet.

Case J

Start over cleanly

Too many hooks, the pod is lost, the SPI is somewhere odd. Reset before the next pass.

  1. Hold TMS ↓ · RCtrl+↓ from any SOI: the SPI goes back to the active steerpoint.
  2. TGP SOI: TMS ↓ · RCtrl+↓ short for INR, then China ↓ · RCtrl+End short to bring the pod back to boresight.
  3. Helmet SOI: TMS ↓ · RCtrl+↓ short to unhook, China ↓ · RCtrl+End short to recage the HDC.

CheckThe SPI symbol sits on the steerpoint and the TGP page reads INR.

Case K

Quick draw with the TGP diamond

A target pops up ahead. You want it in the pod without slewing across the whole screen.

  1. TGP ON, warm, page in A-G. Hold Coolie → · RShift+→ to make the TGP page SOI.
  2. China ↓ · RCtrl+End short: the pod goes to boresight, along the aircraft axis.
  3. Look through the HUD: the TGP diamond shows where the pod looks. Fly it onto the target.
  4. TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short: the pod holds the target in AREA.
  5. Zoom with DMS ↑ · RAlt+↑, fine-tune with Slew · Num 8/2/4/6, then TMS ↑ · RCtrl+↑ short again on a vehicle for POINT.

CheckThe page reads AREA or POINT and the diamond stays on the target while you turn.

Case L

Mark a point from the HUD and send the pod there

You see a position through the HUD, helmet off, and want to keep it and look at it with the pod.

  1. Press Coolie ↑ · RShift+↑ until the HUD is SOI. The green TDC square appears on the flight path.
  2. Move it with Slew · Num 8/2/4/6 onto the spot and release: it stays on the ground.
  3. TMS → · RCtrl+→ short: a markpoint (WP Alpha, WP Bravo...) appears there.
  4. DMS ↑ · RAlt+↑ or DMS ↓ · RAlt+↓ until the HUD shows that markpoint as the steerpoint. With no other SPI, it is now the SPI.
  5. Hold China ↑ · RCtrl+Home: the TGP and the Maverick slew to it. If the SPI came from a sensor, hold TMS ↓ · RCtrl+↓ first to bring it back to the steerpoint.

CheckThe steerpoint name in the HUD is your markpoint and the TGP video shows the spot you marked.

Case M

Keep the target when your wing gets in the way

You orbit the target with the pod on it. In the turn, the wing or a store blocks the pod.

  1. Watch the track field: it changes to INR-A (area) or INR-P (vehicle). The laser letter shows M.
  2. Do not slew and do not press TMS. The pod keeps the point by itself.
  3. Roll out or change the bank until the pod sees the ground again.
  4. If you were lasing, press the laser key again once the M is gone.

CheckThe track field is back to AREA or POINT on the same target and the M is gone.
